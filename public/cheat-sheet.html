Precision RFQ Cheat Sheet

Twelve precision-machining estimating considerations across pre, in-process, and post-machining. Tape it to the estimating desk.

Every line below is a consideration that catches a senior estimator on a careful Tuesday-night read — and gets missed on a Friday rush. Run a 60-second check against this list before any RFQ leaves the estimating desk.

§ Before the first chip hits

Material, fixturing, datum strategy

01 · Material spec vs material cert
Cert traceability is a sourcing constraint, not just a material grade

When the print or PO calls for a specific grade (e.g., "4140 PH") AND requires mill-cert traceability to the heat lot, your supplier must provide both. If they can't, you re-source, switch suppliers, or the lot is non-conforming at receiving inspection — too late to recover the margin.

02 · Raw stock allowance
Forgings, castings, weldments, hot-rolled stock need cleanup margin

Non-stock-shape inputs come with scale, distortion, parting lines, or weld-bead geometry that has to be roughed off before semi-finish. Quoting nominal stock dimensions on these inputs leads to under-billed roughing and surprise scrap. Add a cleanup allowance to the stock line, even if the print shows finished dimensions only.

03 · Datum strategy
How the print locates the part vs how the shop will hold it

The drawing's datum scheme (A‑B‑C, primary/secondary/tertiary) tells the inspector how the part should measure. It may not match how your shop can practically hold the part during machining. Mismatch means custom soft-jaws, multiple setups, or a fixture NRE — frequently absorbed by the estimator. Resolve before quoting setup time.

04 · First-time fixture / soft-jaw NRE
Complex shapes and 5-axis parts often need workholding NRE

First-time complex parts (irregular shapes, weldments, thin walls, 5-axis features) often need custom soft-jaws, dedicated fixtures, or vacuum chucks. These are real engineering hours. Quote NRE as a separate line — or amortize it explicitly across the order quantity. Don't bury it in the per-piece price for a one-off.

§ While the part is on the machine

Tolerance, sequence, in-process

05 · UOS title block vs explicit GD&T
Title-block tolerances are the default — feature-level GD&T overrides them

"Unless Otherwise Specified" tolerances in the title block (e.g., ±0.005") are the fallback. Specific features may have tighter explicit GD&T (true position, runout, cylindricity, profile). Don't assume UOS applies everywhere. The expensive ones are usually buried in a callout or a side view.

06 · Tolerance stack-up across feature chains
When features are dimensioned in a chain, tolerances stack additively

Datum → feature A → feature B → feature C, each with ±0.001", gives you ±0.003" at the end. If the print specifies an end-feature tighter than the chain allows, it's either a print error or it implies a different datum strategy (e.g., direct from primary datum). Verify before quoting — catching this in production is six figures.

07 · Stress relief on long parts
Long parts (L:D > 8:1) need stress relief between rough and finish

Long shafts and slender parts without an explicit stress-relief callout typically need one anyway. Skipping it = warpage after final machining = scrap or rework. The callout may not be on the drawing — it may be in customer process specs you weren't sent. Confirm the customer's expectation before pricing.

08 · Heat-treat sequence
Heat treat must precede finish grinding — never the reverse

Grinding a part that's then sent for heat treat distorts the ground surface. If the print shows "heat treat" after "grind," it's either a sequence error or the print expects pre-HT soft grind + post-HT finish grind (two grind operations). Verify which — they price very differently.

§ After the part leaves the machine

Outside ops, finishing, lead time, inspection

09 · Surface finish (Ra) implies an operation
Each Ra band points to a finishing operation the print may not scope

Ra ≤ 32 µin: achievable by careful milling/turning. Ra ≤ 16 µin: implies grinding. Ra ≤ 8 µin: implies honing or lapping. Ra ≤ 4 µin: implies superfinish, polish, or lapping. The Ra callout *implies* an operation that may not be explicitly listed on the routing. Read every Ra value and confirm an op exists that can hit it.

10 · Pre-coat / pre-plate dimensional compensation
Any coating that adds material requires pre-coat dimensional comp

Approximate per-side buildups: anodize 0.0001–0.0005", hard-coat anodize 0.0005–0.001", electroless nickel 0.0002–0.0005", chrome plate 0.001–0.003" (varies wildly), zinc 0.0002–0.0008", paint 0.001–0.005". Threads, bores, slip-fits, and pilot diameters are most affected. Calculate pre-coat dimensions and machine to those — or scrap the lot at gage inspection.

11 · Deburr & break edge
"Break all sharp edges .005-.015" R" is a real operation, not a freebie

A part with 40 features needs 40 deburr touches. Tumble or vibratory finish covers the easy ones — not interior edges, threads, or critical bores. On medical, aerospace, or food-contact parts, manual hand-deburr can run 10–20 minutes per part — frequently unscoped in the original quote. Estimate it explicitly.

12 · Outside-op lead time stack
Each external hand-off compounds — multiply, don't add

Each outside operation adds shipping (1–3 days each way) + vendor turnaround (3–10 days) + minimum lot charges. Three outside ops is rarely 9 days; it's typically 15–21 days. Plus FAI/PPAP (if required by the print or PO) adds another 5–10 business days before production can ramp. Get firm lead-time + min-lot quotes from every vendor before committing to delivery.
